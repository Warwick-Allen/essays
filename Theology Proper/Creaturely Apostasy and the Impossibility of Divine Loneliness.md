  A companion essay argued that the claim that God created the world to
  relieve loneliness fails both exegetically and metaphysically.  The present
  essay takes up the question that essay reserved: whether, given creation,
  God can suffer loneliness when His creatures turn away from Him.  The
  question is dialectically harder than its predecessor, because the canon
  abundantly ascribes to God affective responses to apostasy, and because this
  essay concedes those ascriptions at full value, arguing within a doctrinal
  framework that affirms genuine divine emotion and real though non-essential
  change while denying every divine deficiency.  Distinguishing the
  vulnerability thesis (that apostasy can render God lonely) from the
  actuality thesis (that it has done so), the essay contends that the former
  fails on convergent grounds.  Exegetically, the canon's repertoire of divine
  responses to apostasy, read at maximal value, yields grief, jealousy,
  yearning, and compassion, but never loneliness; it assigns the lexicon of
  desertion and bereavement exclusively to creatures while rendering divine
  aloneness as sufficiency; and it records the incarnate Son's threefold
  denial that He is alone, the last made in the teeth of comprehensive
  creaturely desertion.  Analytically, loneliness, unlike grief, is
  constituted by a reflexive deficit, so the concession of divine grief cannot
  be parlayed into divine loneliness.  Trinitarianly and providentially,
  neither the fellowship intrinsic to God's life nor the creaturely fellowship
  He has decreed for Himself can be diminished by apostasy.  Modally,
  loneliness is an imposed state, whereas every affective state God admits is
  freely undertaken.  The Christological limit cases of Gethsemane and the cry
  of dereliction are examined and shown not to supply the thesis, and a survey
  of passibilist theologies shows that even they construe divine suffering
  transitively, as suffering for the creature.  God is grieved by apostasy; He
  cannot be bereaved by it; and the asymmetry is the shape of grace.

## 1. Introduction

A companion essay examined the suggestion that God created the world because He was lonely, and returned a negative verdict: the affective thesis (L1), that God apart from creation experienced loneliness, is without exegetical support and inconsistent with every serious construal of divine perfection, and the aetiological thesis (L2), that creation remedies that state, collapses with it.[^1] That essay, however, confined its attention to God considered apart from creation. Its treatment of the biblical anthropopathisms observed that grief, jealousy, wrath, and compassion are, in the canon, covenantal responses to an already-existing creation, and it deliberately left unexamined the further question those responses raise. The present essay takes that question up: given creation, and given the canonical datum that creatures do turn away from their Creator, can God now suffer loneliness? The question is not hypothetical. Apostasy, taken here in a broad sense embracing the Adamic fall, Israel's covenant-breaking, individual unbelief, and final impenitence, is among the most heavily documented facts of the biblical narrative, and the post-creational form of the "lonely God" claim circulates as readily as the ante-creational form did. The pining God of some devotional rhetoric, who misses His wandering children as a bereaved parent misses her own, and whose calls to return are in part appeals for His own relief, is a fixture of revivalist and pastoral appeal, and the texts on which the figure trades are genuinely biblical.

The claim is again best analysed as a conjunction of two distinguishable theses, numbered in continuity with the companion essay.

3. **The vulnerability thesis (L3).** God, given creation, is so related to His creatures that their turning away from Him can render Him lonely.
4. **The actuality thesis (L4).** God is, or at some time has been, lonely in consequence of creatures turning away from Him.

L4 entails L3: what has occurred is possible. The argumentative burden accordingly falls on L3, whose refutation carries L4 with it *a fortiori*, in mirror image of the companion essay's procedure with L1 and L2. The definition of loneliness is carried over unchanged from that essay (§2.1 below), so that the two essays address a single predicate throughout.

Three asymmetries prevent the present question from being settled by the earlier answer. The first is evidential. Against L1 it could be observed that the argument from anthropopathism must first invent the datum it proposes to interpret, since the canon nowhere attributes loneliness, or anything adjacent to it, to God apart from creation. No such economy is available here: the canon ascribes to God a rich repertoire of affective responses to apostasy, from the grief of Genesis 6:6 to the lament of Luke 19:41, and an argument that ignored or minimised that repertoire would deserve to fail. The second asymmetry is dogmatic. The companion essay's central formal argument (its §4.2) ran through pure actuality and strict immutability. The present essay argues within a doctrinal framework, set out in §2.2, that affirms genuine divine emotion and real though non-essential change, and it therefore cannot simply reuse that argument; it must find another route to the same terminus. The third asymmetry is dialectical. The earlier essay's survey of process, passibilist, and open theologies found that even the tradition's most determined revisionists decline L1. On the post-creational question those same theologies affirm divine suffering emphatically, so the boundary test must be re-run, and with more care (§7).

The thesis defended is that L3 fails even so. Loneliness is not merely an emotion; it is an emotion constituted by a deficit, an unmet need for relational goods belonging to the subject's own welfare, and the deficit it requires cannot obtain in God, whether really or, since loneliness is defined over a *perceived* discrepancy, in God's own construal of His situation. The essay accordingly concedes, both for the sake of argument and by conviction, the full prima facie force of the texts that ascribe emotion to God, and contends that the concession yields grief, jealousy, yearning, and compassion, while yielding loneliness at no point. The argument is thereby conducted on the terrain most favourable to the thesis it opposes: if L3 fails on premisses hospitable to divine passibility, it fails on all premisses. The method remains that of a cumulative case, with exegesis, dogmatics, and philosophical theology treated as mutually informing.[^2]

## 2. Definitions and Dogmatic Location

### 2.1 The definition of loneliness, carried over and sharpened

The companion essay adopted the standard account in the social-psychological literature, on which loneliness is the aversive experience arising from a perceived discrepancy between a subject's desired and achieved social relations, and drew out its three constitutive features: an intentional object (a desire for companionship, fellowship, or intimacy), a privation (the desired good is not possessed, or not in the desired degree), and aversive affect (loneliness is suffered, which distinguishes it from solitude).[^3] Two features of the definition, present in it all along, now require explicit statement because the present question turns on them.

The first is reflexivity. The discrepancy that constitutes loneliness is a discrepancy in the subject's *own* social relations; the good whose absence it registers is a good *for the subject*, an ingredient of the subject's relational welfare. Sadness at the thought that another person lacks fellowship is not loneliness but compassion; the lonely subject is the party deprived. The second is the perception clause. Since the definition is framed over a perceived discrepancy, a subject is lonely only if there is a deficit in his relational goods or he construes there to be one. Loneliness therefore has exactly two possible grounds, a real deficit and a misperceived one, and an argument that closes both is complete.
Both features bear weight in §4.

As in the companion essay, the strict sense is the sense addressed. If "loneliness" is softened until it names only God's delight in fellowship or His desire that the wanderer return, then L3 dissolves into doctrines no one disputes, and the thesis loses the pathos that gives it its rhetorical life. The interesting claim, and the one examined here, is that creaturely desertion deprives God of something and that He suffers the deprivation.

### 2.2 The dogmatic framework of this essay

The present essay argues within a doctrinal framework whose commitments are set out in a separate statement of faith and are here presupposed; the statement is cited parenthetically by article number.[^4] Four commitments matter for the argument.

1. **Genuine divine emotion.** God truly experiences emotion, including love, joy, and grief, in response to what happens among His creatures, and the biblical ascriptions of such emotion are true reports of God's own inner life rather than mere figures or accommodations (arts 1–2). The classical doctrine of divine impassibility in its strict form, on which God cannot be affected by His creatures in any respect, is accordingly declined (art. 3).[^5]
2. **No divine deficiency.** God's emotion is never occasioned by ignorance or by powerlessness; His love and desire for creatures measure the strength of His love and not any unmet need; His emotional responses are always rightly proportioned to their objects and never misdirected; and His grief coexists with, and never diminishes, His secure and fundamental blessedness (arts 4–9).
3. **Immutability of character.** The scriptural declarations that God does not change (Malachi 3:6; James 1:17) assert the constancy of His character, moral will, and covenant faithfulness, not the absence of all real change; occurrent joy or grief, and above all the incarnation, are real changes that leave the divine nature and character untouched (arts 11–15).
4. **Freedom.** Every change God undergoes, including His being moved to grief or joy, is freely undertaken by God Himself and never imposed upon Him by anything outside Himself (art. 16).

The relation of this framework to the companion essay should be stated candidly. That essay's central formal argument deployed *actus purus* and strict immutability, premisses the framework does not supply; the present essay therefore does not lean on it. But the earlier essay's §4.3 anticipated exactly this situation, showing that the ante-creational result stands on weaker and less contestable grounds, namely divine beatitude and trinitarian plenitude, both of which the framework affirms without reservation. The present essay extends those routes to the post-creational case. The procedure has a dialectical benefit worth naming: a refutation of L3 that presupposed strict impassibility would persuade no one not already persuaded, since the vulnerability thesis would simply be excluded by fiat at the level of first principles. A refutation mounted from premisses that grant genuine divine emotion demonstrates something stronger, that loneliness is excluded not by a general embargo on divine affect but by the specific structure of loneliness itself.

### 2.3 Grief and loneliness distinguished by their formal objects

The affections are individuated by their formal objects: fear regards a future evil as hard to avoid, hope a future good as hard but possible to attain, sorrow a present evil, joy a present good. The taxonomy is Aquinas's, and it can be borrowed without his conclusions. Aquinas himself, holding that sorrow imports a defect in its subject, ascribes sorrow to God only metaphorically or by its effects, and his treatment of mercy states the classical position exactly: to sorrow over another's misery does not belong to God, but to dispel that misery belongs to Him most of all.[^6] The framework adopted here affirms, beyond Aquinas, that God truly sorrows (art. 1); what it borrows from him is the method of individuating affections by their objects, not the impassibilist result.

Applying the method to the two affections at issue yields the distinction on which this essay turns. The formal object of grief is an evil afflicting what one loves. Nothing in that specification requires that the evil afflict the griever: one may grieve wholly for another, over the other's ruin, dishonour, or loss, and such grief takes nothing for granted about any deficit in the griever's own welfare. Grief is therefore connected to deficit in its subject *contingently*: a needy subject may grieve, and so may a subject lacking nothing. The formal object of loneliness, by contrast, is fixed by the definition of §2.1 as the subject's own unpossessed relational good. Loneliness is connected to deficit in its subject *analytically*: remove the reflexive privation and what remains is no longer loneliness but some other affection, compassion, longing for another's good, or grief proper. It follows that "grieving from fullness" is a coherent notion, and, if the framework of §2.2 is correct, one canonically instantiated on nearly every page of the prophets, whereas "lonely from fullness" is a contradiction in terms.

This asymmetry is the hinge of the whole essay. The framework's concession that God genuinely grieves cannot be parlayed into the conclusion that God can be lonely without an additional premiss, namely that God has an unmet need for relational goods of His own, and that premiss the framework expressly denies (art. 7). The remainder of the essay argues that the denial is not an arbitrary stipulation but is forced, exegetically (§3), trinitarianly, providentially, and modally (§4).

## 3. The Scriptural Witness to God Forsaken

### 3.1 The affective repertoire of the apostasy texts

The canon's testimony to God's response to apostasy is extensive, and the framework of §2.2 requires that it be read at full value. The LORD "regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart" (Genesis 6:6); Israel's idolatry "provoked him to jealousy" and to vexation (Deuteronomy 32:16, 21; cf. Exodus 34:14); in the days of the judges "he became impatient over the misery of Israel" (Judges 10:16); the rebellious house "grieved his Holy Spirit" (Isaiah 63:10), as the church is warned not to "grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Ephesians 4:30). Alongside grief and jealousy stands yearning compassion: "How can I give you up, O Ephraim?... My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender" (Hosea 11:8); "Is Ephraim my dear son?... my heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him" (Jeremiah 31:20). Alongside these stands pleading appeal: "I spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people" (Isaiah 65:2, quoted of God at Romans 10:21); "What wrong did your fathers find in me that they went far from me?" (Jeremiah 2:5); "O my people, what have I done to you? How have I wearied you? Answer me!" (Micah 6:3). The incarnate Son laments over the city: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matthew 23:37; cf. Luke 19:41–44). God seeks the wanderer, from the first "Where are you?" of Genesis 3:9 to the shepherd, the woman, and the father of Luke 15, and "the Father is seeking such people to worship him" (John 4:23). And God rejoices over recovery: there is "joy in heaven over one sinner who repents" (Luke 15:7), a rejoicing the framework insists is God's own true joy and not merely that of angelic bystanders (art. 10); "he will rejoice over you with gladness... he will exult over you with loud singing" (Zephaniah 3:17).

Two observations follow from this survey, and the first parallels the companion essay's finding about the ante-creational case while considerably strengthening it. Read at maximal value, without accommodationist discounting of any kind, the repertoire yields grief, jealousy, provocation, yearning, compassion, appeal, lament, seeking, and joy. It does not yield loneliness. The point deserves emphasis precisely because the present essay's hermeneutic maximises the yield of these texts: a reading that takes Genesis 6:6 and Hosea 11:8 as true reports of the divine inner life extracts from the canon everything that can be extracted, and what it extracts still does not include the datum L3 and L4 require. The strongest text, Jeremiah 31:20, is instructive in exactly this respect: the divine yearning it reports resolves in the same breath into "I will surely have mercy on him", so that even at its most visceral the canonical portrait presents a yearning whose intentional object is Ephraim's restoration, not the relief of a divine want.

The second observation concerns the *structure* of the affections attributed, and it is developed in the next two subsections: the canon not only omits divine loneliness but systematically casts God, in every apostasy text, in the role that excludes it.

### 3.2 The direction of loss

When the prophets state what apostasy costs, and whom, the accounting is uniform. "My people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). The deserter forsakes a fountain; the fountain does not run dry; the loss, total and self-inflicted, is the deserter's. The same oracle frames the desertion as ingratitude and folly rather than as deprivation of the divine subject: "Can a virgin forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire? Yet my people have forgotten me days without number" (Jeremiah 2:32). The covenant-lawsuit genre fixes the forensic posture: in the *rîb* of Micah 6:1–8, of Isaiah 1:2–3, and of the vineyard song of Isaiah 5:1–7, God appears as plaintiff, the wronged party with standing to accuse, and His complaint is the complaint of the exhaustive supplier: "What more was there to do for my vineyard, that I have not done in it?" (Isaiah 5:4). Job states the underlying principle with philosophical bluntness: "If you have sinned, what do you accomplish against him?... Your wickedness concerns a man like yourself, and your righteousness a son of man" (Job 35:6, 8; cf. 22:2–3). What apostasy subtracts, it subtracts from the apostate.

Divine jealousy, the canon's own preferred category for God's response to violated exclusivity, confirms the pattern rather than disturbing it. Jealousy is the affect of a *right* violated, not of a *need* unmet; a claim can be trampled without the claimant's welfare being diminished, and the *qinʾâ* of the LORD is covenant zeal, protective of an exclusive bond whose entire benefit runs to the creature. The nuptial texts, as the companion essay showed in another connection (§7 there), present the divine husband invariably as the supplier of a destitute and faithless partner and never as the needy party; Hosea's wronged husband responds to desertion not by pining but by providing, hedging the wayward wife's path for her protection and then alluring her into the wilderness to speak tenderly to her, for her restoration (Hosea 2:6–23). Where God withdraws, the withdrawal is disciplinary and purposive, "I will return again to my place, until they acknowledge their guilt and seek my face" (Hosea 5:15), and where the language of forsaking occurs with God as subject, He is the one who forsakes in judgement (Jeremiah 12:7), never the one bereft.

### 3.3 The lexicon of desertion belongs to the creature

The observation can be pressed to the level of vocabulary. Biblical Hebrew possesses a developed lexicon of solitude, desertion, and bereavement, and the canon uses it liberally: the sufferer is "lonely and afflicted" (Psalm 25:16), "like a lonely sparrow on the housetop" (Psalm 102:7); the ruined city "sits alone" (*bādād*, Lamentations 1:1); Zion is "like a wife deserted and grieved in spirit" (Isaiah 54:6), was named "Forsaken" (*ʿăzûbâ*, Isaiah 62:4), and describes herself as "bereaved and barren" (Isaiah 49:21).[^7] Every one of these applications is to creatures. Most striking of all, the deserted-and-grieving-wife figure, the closest image the canon possesses to the state L3 contemplates, is applied to Israel, with God cast as the husband who recalls and restores her: "For the LORD has called you like a wife deserted and grieved in spirit... For a brief moment I deserted you, but with great compassion I will gather you" (Isaiah 54:6–7). The canon, that is, does not merely happen to omit divine loneliness; it distributes the relevant vocabulary with perfect consistency, so that solitude-as-privation is creaturely.

And when Scripture does predicate aloneness of God, the sense is inverted into its contrary. "I have trodden the winepress alone, and from the peoples no one was with me... I looked, but there was no one to help; I was appalled, but there was no one to uphold; so my own arm brought me salvation" (Isaiah 63:3, 5; cf. 59:16). Divine aloneness here is unassisted sufficiency: the absence of helpers is registered, and answered from within. "The LORD alone guided him, and there was no foreign god with him" (Deuteronomy 32:12) makes aloneness the exclusivity of His agency. The lexical pattern is thus double: solitude as privation is assigned to creatures without exception, and solitude as predicated of God signifies sufficiency without exception. A thesis that requires the canon's silence to conceal the opposite of what the canon everywhere says has passed beyond the reach of exegetical support.

### 3.4 The Johannine denials

One further datum outranks the rest, because in it the scenario L3 contemplates is not approached but realised, and realised in extremis. Three times in the Fourth Gospel the incarnate Son asserts that He is not alone (*monos*): "it is not I alone who judge, but I and the Father who sent me" (John 8:16); "he who sent me is with me. He has not left me alone" (John 8:29); and, climactically, on the night of His betrayal, "Behold, the hour is coming, indeed it has come, when you will be scattered, each to his own home, and will leave me alone. Yet I am not alone, for the Father is with me" (John 16:32).[^8] The third saying is spoken in the teeth of a prophecy of total creaturely desertion: every disciple will abandon Him, and did (Matthew 26:56). Here, at the one point in the canon where God incarnate is comprehensively deserted by His creatures, the subject Himself denies the predicate, and denies it on precisely the trinitarian ground the companion essay developed: the Father's presence. John 16:32 thus functions for the post-creational question as Acts 17:25 functioned for the ante-creational one, a direct dominical assertion placed at the exact point of maximal pressure. The verse does not merely fail to support L3; it is as close to an explicit denial of L3 as a narrative canon could be expected to contain.

[^8]: On the force of these sayings see D. A. Carson, *The Gospel According to John* (Leicester: Apollos; Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1991), ad loc.

## 4. The Argument from the Structure of Loneliness

### 4.1 The formal argument

The central argument can now be stated, in mirror image of the companion essay's §4.2 but from the weaker premisses that essay's §4.3 identified.

1. Loneliness is the aversive experience arising from a perceived discrepancy between the subject's desired and achieved social relations; the deficit it registers is reflexive, a privation in and for the subject (§2.1).
2. Hence God is lonely, or can be, only if either (a) a deficit in God's own relational goods obtains or can obtain, or (b) God perceives, or can perceive, such a deficit where none obtains.
3. No such deficit obtains or can obtain: the fellowship intrinsic to God's life is triune and inviolable (§4.2); the creaturely fellowship God has willed for Himself is willed freely, from fullness, and secured infallibly (§4.3); and God's blessedness is fundamental and undiminished by His grief (art. 9).
4. God cannot perceive such a deficit where none obtains: His emotion is never occasioned by ignorance, and His affective construals are never misdirected or disproportionate (arts 5, 8; cf. Hebrews 4:13).
5. Therefore God neither is nor can be lonely, however far creaturely apostasy runs: L3 is false, and L4 falls with it.

Premisses 1 and 2 are analytic, unpacking the definition; premiss 4 is supplied directly by the framework's doctrine of divine knowledge and of rightly proportioned affect, and closes the perception horn that the discrepancy model itself opens. The substantive work falls on premiss 3, whose three clauses the next three subsections supply. It should be noticed that no premiss invokes impassibility, pure actuality, or strong immutability: the argument is available in its entirety to a theology that affirms genuine divine emotion.

### 4.2 The trinitarian ground

The companion essay's §5 established that the relations constitutive of God's life are eternal and wholly *ad intra*: the Father was never without His Word and His Spirit, the mutual love of the persons is the eternal actuality of the divine life, and Richard of St Victor's doctrine of the *condilectus* converts the intuition that love requires an object into an argument for the Trinity rather than for the necessity of the world. Nothing in that argument was indexed to the ante-creational case, and it transfers to the post-creational case without remainder. Apostasy, however extensive, subtracts nothing from the communion of Father, Son, and Spirit; a world in total revolt would leave the divine fellowship exactly what it eternally is. The relational good whose absence loneliness would register is therefore a good God cannot lack, and cannot lack because, in the order of persons, He eternally *is* it. John 16:32 is the dominical enactment of the point: deserted by every creature, the Son locates His company where it had been from before the foundation of the world (John 17:5, 24). Loneliness requires an empty chair at the subject's own table; the doctrine of the Trinity confesses a table at which no chair is ever empty.

### 4.3 The providential ground

The harder question concerns the fellowship God wills with creatures. God has, on any reading of the canon, willed such fellowship; creatures have, on any reading of the canon, refused Him; can the refusal not defeat the will, and so open on God's side the very discrepancy between desired and achieved relations that the definition requires? The answer turns on a distinction as old as the exegesis of the problem itself, between the will of God's decree and the will of His command, *voluntas beneplaciti* and *voluntas signi*.[^9] God's revealed will and desire that the wicked turn is real, is truly spurned by the impenitent, and is truly grieved over: "Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, declares the Lord GOD, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23); "Turn back, turn back from your evil ways, for why will you die, O house of Israel?" (Ezekiel 33:11).[^10] But the fellowship God has decreed *for Himself*, a people for His own possession, a bride, a dwelling, is not defeasible by creaturely refusal. "All that the Father gives me will come to me" (John 6:37), and it is the Father's will that the Son "should lose nothing of all that he has given me" (6:39); Christ will "present the church to himself in splendor" (Ephesians 5:27); the new covenant undertakes not only God's fidelity but, remarkably, the people's: "I will make with them an everlasting covenant, that I will not turn away from doing good to them. And I will put the fear of me in their hearts, that they may not turn from me" (Jeremiah 32:40; cf. 31:33–34).[^11] That is, the covenant of grace internalises a guarantee against the very apostasy L3 requires: the fellowship God wills for Himself is a fellowship whose perseverance He Himself secures.

Nor has the guarantee ever lacked present instantiation. At the high-water mark of Israel's apostasy, when Elijah supposed himself the last worshipper alive, the divine reply was "I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal" (Romans 11:4, citing 1 Kings 19:18), and Paul generalises the pattern: "So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace" (11:5). "The LORD's portion is his people" (Deuteronomy 32:9), and there has been no moment of covenant history at which the portion was empty. The decreed terminus gathers all of this: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" (Revelation 21:3).

The consequence for the formal argument is exact. In the reflexive sense that loneliness requires, the "achieved" term of the discrepancy can never fall below the "desired" term, because what God wills for Himself He infallibly has and will have. What He commands of creatures and they refuse, He grieves over, and the grief's intentional object lies wholly on the creature's side: the pathos of Ezekiel 33:11 is spent entirely on the hearer's ruin, "why will *you* die?".
The spurning of the preceptive will thus yields precisely the affection the framework affirms and §2.3 distinguishes, namely grief for the creature; it cannot yield the affection L3 requires, deprivation of the divine subject.

### 4.4 The modal ground

The framework's final commitment supplies a third and independent ground. Every change God undergoes, including His being moved to grief, is freely undertaken by God Himself and never imposed upon Him from without (art. 16). Loneliness, however, is by its nature an imposition: the lonely do not elect their state; it is suffered as the unwilled effect of circumstance, which is what its aversiveness registers. Even God's grief, on the framework, is sovereign grief. Ephesians 4:30 permits creatures to grieve the Spirit only because God has covenantally bound Himself so as to admit it, and the verse anchors the very possibility in the seal "for the day of redemption": the openness to being grieved is enclosed within the certainty of the end. No creature can inflict upon God a state He has not willed to admit.

Could God, then, freely undertake loneliness as He freely undertakes grief? He could not, and the reason is the analytical asymmetry of §2.3. Grief can be admitted from fullness, because its formal object is another's evil; loneliness cannot be admitted from fullness, because what would be admitted is precisely a deficit in oneself, and a deficit one does not have cannot be acquired by fiat while remaining what the definition requires, an unmet need. For God to feel the apostate's absence as His own unmet need would be to construe as lacking what He possesses in plenitude, and the framework excludes misdirected and disproportionate affect (art. 8) as firmly as it excludes affect occasioned by ignorance (art. 5). The perception horn of premiss 4 accordingly closes from this side as well: an omniscient subject whose situation contains no relational deficit, and whose every affective construal is veridical and proportionate, can neither have the discrepancy loneliness requires nor seem to Himself to have it. What such a subject freely admits, when His covenant is broken, is exactly what the canon reports Him admitting: grief, jealousy, and yearning for the wanderer's return, each of them the affection of fullness wronged, none of them the affection of want.

## 5. The Christological Limit Cases

### 5.1 Gethsemane and the desertion of the disciples

The incarnate Son, in His human nature, possessed creaturely relational goods that could really be withdrawn, and they were. "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me", He said, and returned to find the three asleep: "So, could you not watch with me one hour?" (Matthew 26:38, 40); within hours, "all the disciples left him and fled" (26:56). Nothing in this essay requires, and orthodoxy forbids, any denial that the Word, according to the humanity He assumed, tasted the specifically human ache of desertion in trial. Hebrews invites the affirmation: He was "in every respect... tempted as we are, yet without sin" (4:15), offering "prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears" (5:7). Such sorrow is sinless, being rightly proportioned to a real creaturely privation: human companionship in extremity is a genuine human good, and He was genuinely refused it. On the framework's Chalcedonian articles (arts 17–21), this yields true predicates of the person, the Word was left by His friends and sorrowed at it, without transferring the creaturely deficit to the divine nature, in exactly the way the *communicatio idiomatum* has always regulated.

But the same night supplies the control. Foretelling that very desertion, the Lord denied in advance the predicate it might seem to license: "you... will leave me alone. Yet I am not alone, for the Father is with me" (John 16:32). What He suffered from His friends' failure was the creaturely sorrow of desertion, experienced in the nature capable of it; what He could not suffer, and expressly denied, was bereavement of divine fellowship. Far from evidencing L3, the human loneliness of Christ exhibits its refutation in person: a real creaturely privation, truly felt, within an unbroken and unbreakable divine communion. The one subject who has stood at the exact point L3 describes, God deserted by every creature who owed Him company, is the one subject on record denying that the desertion left Him alone.

### 5.2 The cry of dereliction

There remains the darkest datum in the canon: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, citing Psalm 22:1). Four bounded observations locate it. First, it is not an instance of the scenario L3 contemplates. The desertion of the Son by creatures was complete before Golgotha and had already received its answer in John 16:32; the cry concerns abandonment by *God*, borne in the unrepeatable office of sin-bearing, "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isaiah 53:10), "for our sake he made him to be sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21), "having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). Whatever the dereliction is, it is not creatures inflicting want upon their Maker. Second, the cry is prayer. "My God, my God" addresses the very One whose absence it laments, and it does so in the opening words of a psalm whose movement runs to vindication: "he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him" (Psalm 22:24). The relation persists in the address itself. Third, the church possesses a grammar for this hour, and the framework has already adopted it (art. 20): Cyril's confession that the Word "suffered impassibly", truly suffering as the one subject of the incarnate life while the divine nature is not converted into a diminished thing, of which Calvin's account, that Christ "bore in his soul the tortures of a condemned and forsaken man", is the Reformed articulation.[^12] Fourth, even the modern theology that has made the most of this cry declines to read it as the world bereaving God. Moltmann, for whom the cross is an event of abandonment within God's own trinitarian life, construes it throughout as free self-giving love for the godless, the Father delivering up the Son and the Son delivering up Himself, not as a privation imposed on God by creaturely desertion.[^13] The dereliction therefore supplies grief, forsakenness borne, and love expended to the uttermost; it does not supply L3. Indeed its logic runs the other way: at the cross God takes upon Himself, in the person of the Son, the God-forsakenness that belonged to sinners, precisely so that they should not bear it. The one dereliction in the canon is God bearing ours.

## 6. Objections and Replies

A first objection presses the unmet desire. If God truly desires the apostate's return and is refused, then God has an unmet relational desire, aversively experienced; and an aversively experienced unmet relational desire, the objector concludes, just is loneliness. The reply is the intentional-object analysis of §2.3 applied to the texts of §4.3. The desire in question has the apostate's good as its object, his turning, his life: the whole pathos of "why will you die, O house of Israel?" is aimed at the hearer's ruin. The definition of loneliness, however, is framed over the subject's *own* social relations, a good for the subject's welfare, and God's relational goods in that reflexive sense are triune and decreed-secured (§§4.2–4.3). An unmet desire for another's good, aversively experienced, is not loneliness; it is grief, and grief is conceded. To convert the concession into L3 the objector must add the reflexive premiss, that God needed the apostate for Himself, and that premiss begs the question against art. 7 and against the entire argument of §4. It is worth adding that unmet preceptive desires are the common lot of any theology that admits either reprobation or permitted evil, which is to say of every Christian theology; no one calls the sorrow they occasion "loneliness" until the reflexive deficit is smuggled in, and it is exactly that addition the argument blocks.

A second objection appeals to covenant vulnerability. By binding Himself to a people, it is said, God has staked His own relational welfare on their fidelity, so that their infidelity now can deprive Him. The reply concedes the premiss and denies the inference. The self-binding is real, and freely undertaken (art. 16); it genuinely admits grief, as Ephesians 4:30 shows. But the covenant's own architecture forecloses the bereavement scenario, for the covenant of grace guarantees the very perseverance it requires: "I will put the fear of me in their hearts, that they may not turn from me" (Jeremiah 32:40). God has freely willed a fellowship that He has also freely secured. The exposure His condescension creates is economic, an openness to being grieved within the administration of the covenant, not final, a susceptibility to losing the covenant's end. A suitor may be refused; the divine Husband has sworn the marriage's consummation and underwritten the bride's fidelity Himself.

A third objection runs in the opposite direction: if the outcome is thus secured, the grief is theatre, and the essay purchases divine invulnerability at the price of divine seriousness. The framework has already answered in general terms, since it denies that God's grief is ever occasioned by ignorance or powerlessness while insisting that it is real (arts 4–6); the specific answer lies in the temporal index of the affections. Sorrow regards a present evil, and on the framework's tensed view of time (art. 14) present evils are presently actual whatever the decreed future holds. The creature's present ruin, and the present dishonour done to God's name, are real states of affairs, and God's occurrent grief is their right estimate. A father certain of the prodigal's eventual return may truly grieve each day of the far country, for each day is truly squandered; and for the finally impenitent there is no return, and that loss is real, final, and really grieved. Certainty of the harvest does not falsify sorrow over the famine.

A fourth objection turns the framework's tensed view of time against the argument. Before the consummation, the decreed fellowship of Revelation 21:3 is not yet complete; is there not, then, *now*, a discrepancy between God's desired and achieved relations, and is the interval between promise and fulfilment not precisely a divine loneliness? The reply is threefold. First, the affections are individuated by temporal index as well as by object: sorrow regards present evil, but a future good regarded as certain is the object of joyful expectation, not of aversive privation; it was "for the joy that was set before him" that Christ endured the cross (Hebrews 12:2), and the exultation of Zephaniah 3:17 is spoken over a restoration then still future. Second, present communion is not nil but actual. "The LORD's portion is his people" (Deuteronomy 32:9) has never lacked a referent (§4.3); the risen Lord is with His church "always, to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20); and the indwelling Spirit is the present "guarantee (*arrabōn*) of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it" (Ephesians 1:13–14), a down payment being precisely a present enjoyment of a future estate. The eschaton perfects an existing fellowship; it does not supply an absent one. Third, beneath both stands the triune plenitude, which is not future but eternal. The interval is therefore, on God's side, the interval of patience (2 Peter 3:9) and of anticipation, not of want.

A fifth objection comes from the classical flank. By conceding real occurrent grief, the strict impassibilist will say, the framework has already surrendered divine blessedness, and loneliness is only more of the same; the essay's line between the two is arbitrary.
The reply is that the line is not arbitrary but analytical, and was drawn in §2.3 before any theological weight was placed on it: grief is contingently, loneliness analytically, connected to deficit in the subject, so a framework can admit the first and exclude the second on principle.
Whether the framework is *right* to admit the first is the passibility debate, which this essay, like its companion at the parallel point (§4.3 there), deliberately declines to adjudicate; the essay's task is the internal one of showing that the framework excludes loneliness without strain, and the classical theist loses nothing by the procedure, since on his stronger premisses L3 fails *a fortiori*, as the companion essay's machinery already shows. The two positions disagree about grief; about loneliness they concur; and the concurrence of rival doctrines of God at just this point is itself evidence of how deep the impossibility lies.

## 7. The Passibilist Boundary Revisited

The companion essay's §8 measured the isolation of the "lonely God" thesis by showing that even process, passibilist, and open theologies decline it. For the post-creational question the survey must be re-run, because here those theologies affirm divine suffering emphatically, and a careless observer might suppose that L3 finds its home among them. Inspection of the structure of their claims shows otherwise, and shows it in a way that vindicates the formal-object analysis of §2.3 from the quarter least suspected of classical bias.

Abraham Heschel, whose theology of the divine pathos stands behind much of the modern literature, is emphatic that pathos is not a self-regarding passion but God's concern for man and His stake in human righteousness, a concern Heschel characterises as transitive, having the other as its subject matter; even Heschel's boldest formulations of the divine need of man specify a need that righteousness be done and that history be redeemed, not a hunger for company, and the loneliness his work labours to overcome is, as the title of his earlier book has it, man's.[^14] Terence Fretheim's study of divine suffering in the Old Testament organises its material under a threefold rubric, God suffering *because* of the people's rejection, *with* the suffering people, and *for* the people, a taxonomy other-directed at every node.[^15] Nicholas Wolterstorff's philosophical defence of divine suffering locates that suffering in God's love for a fallen world, sorrow over the world's wounds.[^16] Moltmann's crucified God suffers in the mode of self-giving love for the godless (§5.2 above). The open theists revise providence to secure genuine divine responsiveness and risk, and their God can be disappointed and grieved by creaturely refusal; but the categories of disappointment and grief are once again transitive, and the same authors affirm the fullness of the triune life, as the companion essay documented.[^17]

The pattern is uniform, and its uniformity is the finding. When the theologians most hospitable to divine suffering reached for categories to describe God's experience of a rebellious world, they reached for pathos, grief, pain, and wounded love, and not for want; the reflexive category of loneliness organises none of their accounts. On the map so drawn, the framework of §2.2 sits between the strict impassibilism of a Weinandy and the fuller passibilism of a Wolterstorff or a Fretheim, nearer to positions that style themselves "impassible yet impassioned";[^18] and the point established is that every position on that map, from one edge to the other, possesses principled grounds for refusing L3, and that they are at bottom the same grounds. The deficit loneliness requires is nowhere to be found in God, and the traditions that disagree about nearly everything else in the doctrine of divine emotion agree about that.

## 8. Conclusion

The vulnerability thesis fails on convergent and mutually independent grounds, and fails on premisses chosen for their hospitality to it.
Exegetically, the canon's repertoire of divine responses to apostasy, read at the maximal value a passibilist hermeneutic can assign it,
yields grief, jealousy, yearning, compassion, and joy at recovery, and never loneliness;
the direction of loss in the apostasy texts runs uniformly creature-ward, from the broken cisterns of Jeremiah 2:13 to the bluntness of Job 35:6–8;
the lexicon of desertion and bereavement is distributed without exception to creatures, while divine aloneness, where Scripture predicates it, signifies sufficiency (Isaiah 63:5);
and the incarnate Son, at the one point in history where God stood comprehensively deserted by His creatures,
three times denied that He was alone, the last time naming the trinitarian ground: "the Father is with me" (John 16:32).
Analytically, grief is connected to deficit in its subject only contingently, and loneliness by definition,
so that a framework affirming genuine divine emotion without divine deficiency admits the one and excludes the other on principle rather than ad hoc.
Trinitarianly, the fellowship God is cannot be subtracted from.
Providentially, the fellowship God wills for Himself is secured by the covenant that guarantees even His people's perseverance (Jeremiah 32:40),
while His spurned desire for the impenitent issues in a grief whose whole object is their ruin.
Modally, loneliness is essentially an imposition, and nothing is imposed on God;
nor can a deficit be freely assumed by One who has none, nor misperceived by One who knows all.
And Christologically, the Word tasted creaturely desertion in the nature assumed for the purpose, denied bereavement on trinitarian grounds,
and at the cross bore for creatures a forsakenness that creatures could never have inflicted on God.

The result is not a subtraction from the biblical portrait of divine pathos but a purification of it. A God who could be made lonely by our leaving would grieve, in part, for Himself, and His calls to return would be, in part, appeals for His own relief. The God of the prophets grieves wholly for us: His jealousy defends a bond whose entire benefit is ours; His yearning is for Ephraim's life, not for Ephraim's services; and the tears of the incarnate Son fall over a city that would not know the things that make for its peace. Because apostasy cannot bereave Him, His grief over it is perfectly gracious, sorrow with nothing to gain. The same asymmetry is the sinner's hope. The fountain does not run dry when we hew our cisterns; the hands spread out all the day (Isaiah 65:2) are not the hands of the needy but of the One whose own arm brings salvation (Isaiah 63:5); and the last word of the canon on the matter is not God restored to a company He had lost, but man restored to the dwelling of God (Revelation 21:3), whose fellowship, triune and replete, was never for a moment diminished. He is grieved; He cannot be bereft; and it is precisely because our going takes nothing from Him that His welcome, when we return, gives everything to us.
