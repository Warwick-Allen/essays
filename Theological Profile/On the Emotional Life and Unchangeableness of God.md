---
title:    On the Emotional Life and Unchangeableness of God
subtitle: Statement of Faith
author:   Warwick Allen
date:     August 2026
---

<br>

This statement addresses three interrelated questions: whether God can
genuinely be moved by his creatures, whether such responsiveness is
compatible with his being without any deficiency, and what it means for
Scripture to say that God does not change.  It is not a complete confession
of faith.

God is genuinely alive to his creatures and moved by what moves them, without
thereby being any less perfect, powerful, or unshakeably good than he has
eternally been.

## 1. God Genuinely Experiences Emotion

1.  I believe that God truly experiences emotion—including love, joy, and
    grief—in response to what happens among his creatures, and that this is not
    merely a figure of speech, an anthropomorphism, or an accommodation to human
    limitation, but a true report of God's own inner life.

2.  I hold this because Scripture ascribes emotion to God pervasively and across
    a wide range of literary genres, including narrative (Genesis 6:6),
    prophecy, and epistle (Ephesians 4:30), a pattern too consistent and too
    varied to dismiss as merely figurative.

3.  I therefore reject the classical doctrine of divine impassibility in its
    strict form, which holds that God cannot be affected by his creatures in any
    respect.

## 2. This Emotion Involves No Deficiency

4.  I believe that God can be genuinely moved, and can genuinely grieve,
    while remaining entirely without deficiency—in knowledge, in power, in
    need, or in character.

5.  God's emotion is never occasioned by ignorance; nothing takes God by
    surprise.

6.  God's emotion is never occasioned by powerlessness; his grief over an evil
    he has permitted is not evidence that he lacked the power to prevent it.

7.  God's love and desire for his creatures are not evidence of any lack or
    incompleteness in him.  His valuing someone or something intensely is a
    measure of the strength of his love, not of an unmet need.

8.  God's emotional responses are always rightly proportioned to their objects.
    They are never excessive, misdirected, or capable of overwhelming his
    judgement or purposes.

9.  God's grief coexists with, and never diminishes, his own secure and
    fundamental blessedness, which rests in his eternal life and the certainty
    of his purposes.

10. On this basis, the rejoicing described in Luke 15:7 over a repentant sinner
    is God's own true joy, and not merely the joy of angels or of the redeemed
    observing from a distance.

## 3. God's Unchangeableness Is the Unchangeableness of His Character

11. I believe that when Scripture declares that God does not change
    (Malachi 3:6, James 1:17), it is speaking of the constancy of his character,
    his moral will, and his covenant faithfulness, not making an exhaustive
    claim that nothing about God ever changes in any respect.

12. God's goodness, justice, faithfulness, and love are, and will always remain,
    entirely without variation.

## 4. Real Change Is Compatible With This Unchangeableness

13. I distinguish three kinds of change that might be spoken of in connection
    with God:
    - A change in a merely relational fact about God—for instance, that a
      repentant sinner now stands in a new relationship to him—which requires no
      alteration in God himself.
    - A real, intrinsic change that is not a change in God's nature or
      character—for instance, God's own occurrent joy or grief at a particular
      event, or, most significantly, the incarnation.
    - A change in God's essential nature, character, or moral will, which never
      occurs and cannot occur.

14. I believe the incarnation is a real change of the second kind.  Before a
    particular point in time, the eternal Word did not subsist in a human
    nature; from that point onward, he did.  Because time is genuinely
    tensed—because the past is truly past and the present truly present, rather
    than all moments being equally and timelessly real—this is an objective
    change in what is true of the Word, not merely a shift in perspective on an
    unchanging whole.

15. This change, however momentous, does not touch God's essential nature or
    character.  What is new is the human nature assumed, and the union of that
    nature to the person of the Word; the divine nature itself is not thereby
    altered, diminished, or converted.

16. Every change God undergoes—whether entering into time, assuming a human
    nature, or being moved to grief or joy—is freely undertaken by God himself,
    and never imposed upon him by anything outside himself.  His capacity for
    such change is an expression of his freedom and love, not evidence of
    dependency or incompleteness.

## 5. This Position Is Consistent with the Chalcedonian Formula

17. The Chalcedonian Definition (451) confesses Christ as one person in two
    natures, united "without confusion, without change, without division,
    without separation," and insists that the property of each nature is
    preserved even as both are united in a single person.

18. Of these four qualifications, only "without change" (Greek: *atreptōs*)
    bears directly on the claims made above.  Read in its own context, this
    clause excludes the divine nature being converted into the human nature, or
    altered or diminished by taking humanity to itself; it equally excludes the
    human nature being absorbed into divinity.  It is a claim about the
    integrity of each nature considered in itself, not a claim that the person
    of the Word gains no new true predicates through the union.

19. My claim that the Word came to have a human nature he did not have before is
    a claim of precisely this second, permitted kind.  Something new is truly
    said of the person—that he is now also human, born of Mary, growing in
    wisdom and stature, and in time crucified—while nothing is said to have
    changed in the divine nature itself, which remains, considered in itself,
    exactly what it eternally is.

20. This is the same distinction the tradition already relies upon in the
    doctrine of the communication of attributes (*communicatio idiomatum*), and
    it echoes Cyril of Alexandria's own formula that the Word "suffered
    impassibly": truly suffering as the single subject of the incarnate life,
    while his divine nature remained, in itself, untouched by that suffering.
    My position stands in this same line.  Real change may truly be said of God
    the Son as a person without the divine nature itself being said to change.

21. Nothing in this statement implies any mixture of the two natures into a
    third kind of thing, nor any division of Christ into two persons, nor any
    separation of the human nature from the person of the Word once assumed.
    The remaining three qualifications of the Definition—without confusion,
    without division, without separation—are accordingly not in question here.

22. I recognise one respect in which this statement goes beyond Chalcedon
    rather than merely restating it.  I hold that God's own nature, and not only
    the incarnate person's human experience, is genuinely responsive to his
    creatures: that God grieved over human wickedness (Genesis 6:6) long before
    the incarnation, and not merely by way of the human nature the Word would
    one day assume.  This claim falls outside the scope of the Chalcedonian
    Definition, which addresses the union of two natures in Christ rather than
    the general question of whether God as such can be moved by his creatures.
    It therefore does not contradict the text of Chalcedon, but it does go
    beyond the wider doctrine of divine apatheia that the bishops who wrote it,
    and Cyril himself, generally took for granted.
